§ 01MethodFive steps

An analysis is only finished when a decision changes

A chart nobody acts on is a cost, not an output. So every piece of work below is written the same way: the question that was asked, the data it had to survive, the method, what came out, and what somebody did differently afterwards.

01

Get the question in writing first

Most requests arrive as "can you look at the claims data". That is not a question. I turn it into one that has an answer and an owner, because otherwise the analysis becomes an argument about impressions later.

02

Interrogate the data before trusting it

Row counts against source systems, duplicates, missing periods, fields that changed meaning halfway through the history. Across the insurance work the data came from several business sources with different structures and different quality, so this step regularly took longer than the modelling.

03

Agree what "better" means before building

A number or a test that both sides accept up front. The claims fraud work had no labelled fraud examples to score against, so the measure had to be agreed as silhouette score plus a business reading of what the model actually flagged, before a single model was trained.

04

Start with the boring baseline

A pivot, a threshold, a simple rule. Sometimes that is the whole answer and everyone goes home early. When it is not, it becomes the number the clever method has to beat, which keeps the comparison honest.

05

Hand over something that keeps running

A reusable pipeline instead of a monthly manual export, a dashboard the business reads without me in the room, and a written account of what the analysis cannot tell you. Stating the limits is part of the deliverable, not an apology for it.

Question
Which claims are unusual enough to be worth a human reviewer's time, given that almost none of the historical claims were ever classified as fraudulent or clean?
Data
Claims records from the insurer's core systems, joined to policy and payment fields. The practical constraint was the one that shapes the whole problem: no labelled fraud examples, so nothing supervised was available and no accuracy figure could be quoted.
Method
  • Feature preparation across claim, policy and payment fields, with the data quality checks done first
  • Three unsupervised approaches compared rather than one assumed: Isolation Forest, clustering, and statistical anomaly detection
  • Evaluation agreed in advance as silhouette score, feature importance, and a business reading of what each method flagged
  • A confidence threshold set deliberately, because it decides how much review work the team receives
Finding
The strongest indicators of an unusual claim, in order:
  1. 01Outstanding amount
  2. 02Sum insured
  3. 03Submitted claim amount
  4. 04Paid amount

Ranked by feature importance. Magnitudes withheld, commercially sensitive.

Decision
Claims review stopped being uniform and became risk ordered, with the threshold as the dial between missed cases and reviewer workload. The known limits were written down alongside it: scarce labels, patterns that shift over time, and a need for periodic retraining with expert review alongside the model rather than instead of it.

Question
Which existing customers are a sensible target for a second product, and on what evidence, rather than on whoever the sales team happened to call last?
Data
Customer records integrated across policy, claims and engagement data held in separate business systems, each with its own structure and its own quality problems. Sensitive customer information throughout, so access was controlled and the analysis worked on what it was entitled to see.
Method
  • Integration first: one customer view assembled from several sources before any analysis
  • Data driven segmentation rather than segments inherited from a slide deck
  • Machine learning to surface behaviour patterns and engagement opportunities across segments
  • Output shaped as decision support dashboards, so business users could read it without a technical team
Finding
A clearer picture of how customers actually behave, and segments defined by observed behaviour instead of assumption. The useful part was not the model, it was that the same customer view became reusable for the next question asked.
Decision
Cross sell targeting moved onto the segmentation, and the dashboards became the standing reference the business reads rather than a report produced on request.

Question
Can insurance contract reporting under IFRS 17 be produced the same way every period, without a manual assembly step that nobody can reconstruct afterwards?
Data
Insurance contract data drawn from core systems. This is reporting that reaches a regulator, so the bar is different from an internal dashboard: every figure has to be traceable back to the record it came from.
Method
  • Automated pipeline replacing the manual reporting routine
  • Migrations tested against a copy of production before they ran against it, because a bad one would have reached the regulator
  • Append only treatment of financial records, so a figure is never overwritten and the history survives
  • Data quality monitoring on the inputs, not only checks on the outputs
Finding
The reporting effort moved from repeated manual work each period to a reviewed pipeline run, and, more usefully, the same numbers can be reproduced later on demand.
Decision
Reporting became a repeatable process with an audit trail rather than a periodic scramble, and the finance team's time moved from assembling figures to checking them.

Question
The team was reading text data through a manual report produced periodically. Could they see it as it arrived instead, and would that change anything they do?
Data
Text from inconsistent sources, which is where nearly all the difficulty sat. Unclear and inconsistent records, no shared format, and nothing usable until it had been cleaned and normalised.
Method
  • ETL pipeline to collect and prepare the text for analysis
  • Cleaning and normalisation rules built for records that disagree with each other
  • NLP analysis layer producing the measures the team actually asked about
  • Grafana dashboard as the reading surface, shipped as one live chart first and grown from there
Finding
The reporting lag was the real problem rather than the analysis. Once the pipeline ran continuously the same questions were answerable at the moment they were asked.
Decision
The periodic manual report was retired. The dashboard is the tool the team reads now, and it analyses data in real time rather than in arrears.
Tools
PythonETLNLPGrafana
Question
Where does stock actually go, and why can nobody answer that from the spreadsheets currently in use?
Data
Inventory levels and stock movements previously tracked in spreadsheets. The failure mode is familiar: several copies, no movement history, and no way to reconstruct how a level reached its current value.
Method
  • A movement history as the source of truth, with levels derived from it rather than typed in
  • Reporting built on that history, so any level can be traced back through the movements that produced it
  • Delivered end to end and handed over, including setup that works from a clean clone
Finding
Once movements were recorded rather than levels overwritten, the questions the business could not previously answer became ordinary queries.
Decision
Stock movement, levels and reporting moved into one system and off spreadsheets, and the client runs it without me.
Tools
SQLNode.jsReactReporting
Question
What does it take for transaction data to be trustworthy enough that a reconciliation always balances, rather than usually balancing?
Data
Core banking transaction data, the setting where correctness is not negotiable and a discrepancy is not an interesting anomaly, it is a defect.
Method
  • Backend services and data access for the core banking platform
  • Append only treatment of financial records: a state change is a new row, never an update in place
  • Reconciliation as a property the data model guarantees rather than a report that discovers problems late
Finding
Most reconciliation problems are not analysis problems. They are recorded upstream, at the moment a row is overwritten instead of appended.
Decision
This is the rule I have carried into every dataset since. It is why the insurance reporting work treats financial records the same way.

The threshold is the decision, not the model

Anomaly detection does not tell you which claims are fraudulent. It ranks them, and then somebody has to choose where to cut. That choice is a business decision about how much review capacity exists, and this is the conversation I would rather have with a stakeholder than a conversation about algorithms. Move the slider.
